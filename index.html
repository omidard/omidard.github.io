01 — Work

Models, pipelines and databases

Everything below is public and runnable. The models are browsable in a web viewer, the pipelines are open source, and the databases are citation-backed.

Flagship database First author

BiGG 2026

biggr.org

The next-generation release of the BiGG Models database, the reference repository for genome-scale metabolic models. A searchable home for curated models, universal metabolite and reaction databases, genomes and compartments, cross-linked to CHEBI, KEGG and MetaCyc. I wrote the pangenome-scale model-generation pipeline behind it.

Open biggr.org Paper in preparation
In-silico twins Live browser

panGEMs Browser

omidard.github.io/panGEMs

A web browser over 2,313 E. coli and 2,346 Lactobacillaceae strain-specific genome-scale metabolic models. Each model is a digital twin of one strain: it predicts what that organism can eat, what it secretes, and which of its genes are essential.

EcopanGEM

Pangenome-scale reconstruction of E. coli metabolism

Reconstruction and analysis across roughly 12,000 E. coli strains: the largest strain-resolved metabolic model collection assembled for a single bacterial species.

LactoPanGEM

Pangenome reconstruction of Lactobacillaceae metabolism

Roughly 2,300 models spanning the whole Lactobacillaceae family, predicting species-specific metabolic traits. Published in mSystems.

Pipeline

PanGPSR

Gene to protein to structure to reaction, at pangenome scale

Large-scale reconstruction that carries a gene all the way through its protein and predicted 3D structure to the reaction it catalyzes, so function rests on structure rather than on an annotation label copied from a homolog.

GitHub Reconstruction
Database

GrowthDB

Measured ground truth for metabolic models

Experimental prokaryote growth rates, uptake and secretion rates, with media and culture conditions, all referenced. This is the measurement a model prediction gets scored against, which is what makes the prediction falsifiable.

GitHub Site
Database

Media

Citation-backed growth media, model-ready

A curated library of growth and simulation media, every component mapped to a standard BiGG exchange reaction and traced to its source. Part of a validation-data suite for genome-scale models.

GitHub Site
Research

Phylon decomposition

Non-negative matrix factorization of the E. coli pangenome

Decomposing a pangenome with NMF pulls out latent structure, phylons, that no other method surfaced: signals nobody imagined were recoverable from gene presence and absence alone. This is the clearest evidence I have that machine learning does not just speed up genomics, it sees things in it we could not.

Under review
ResearchScience Advances 2026

The genetic basis of metabolic function

Millions of genes, thousands of genomes

The same enzyme activity can be encoded by genes that share almost no sequence. I search millions of genes across thousands of genomes, test whether each is genuinely functional rather than merely annotated, and map the full space of distinct genetic codes that arrive at one and the same chemistry.

03 — About

Why I do this

Ten years ago I was the first person in my department to do computational biology. There was no course to take and nobody to learn it from, so I taught it to myself.

I did that because I believed a new Darwin would come out of computational biology, for one reason: computation was the only way to look at life at a scale where the real patterns become visible, and nothing else could give us that perception.

A decade later my conviction has changed shape. The data is here. What biology lacks is a reasoning system that can work at this scale, and that is what machine learning, and now language models, change. Decomposing bacterial genomes with non-negative matrix factorization recovers signals nobody imagined were recoverable. And the agentic research system I built on Claude Code mines public databases at a resolution nobody had the throughput to reach. The knowledge was already sitting there. Nobody could get to it.

I did not arrive at computation from computer science. I trained in general biology, molecular biology and biotechnology first, isolated bacteria at the bench, and took seven strains from a flask to commercial production at a startup. That is why I know where a prediction meets reality, and where it does not.

Ph.D. Systems Biology, DTU · trained in the group of Prof. Bernhard O. Palsson
